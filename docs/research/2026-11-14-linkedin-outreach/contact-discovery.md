# Finding the right person for one job (2026-11-14)

How to find, in about 5 minutes, the one person most likely to move a job you applied to:
the recruiter who owns it, the hiring manager, the founder of a small company, or someone who
could refer you. Germany has its own page ([germany-strategy.md](germany-strategy.md)); the
messages are in [message-templates.md](message-templates.md).

How this was checked: 20 live postings from the app's shortlist and visa list were read on
2026-11-14 (UK, Netherlands, Ireland, UAE, remote), with vendor help pages and studies for the
rest. No LinkedIn page was opened and nothing was sent. Evidence grades: **A** = a study or a
platform's own large dataset, **B** = a vendor's dataset (mostly sales tools), **C** = opinion or
a few examples. Outside numbers are guesses for you to test, not targets.

## The short version

- **Read the posting first.** It rarely names a person (1 of 20), but it often names the
  *role* that owns the opening ("you'll report to our Lead Backend Engineer", 7 of 20) or the
  team (7 of 20). That gives you one precise LinkedIn search instead of five vague ones.
- **The job poster on LinkedIn is the best free lead.** If the job is on LinkedIn, "Meet the
  hiring team" names the person who posted it. 2 of the 10 people who accepted in week one were
  job posters.
- **Fewer searches per job.** LinkedIn caps people searches per month (it does not publish the
  number, and Premium Career is reported to keep the cap). The skill allows up to 6 searches per
  company. At 25-30 jobs a week that can hit the cap. Aim for 1-2.
- **A referral can be worth more than any message, but only from someone who knows you, and
  only before you apply.** In one big hiring dataset, 40% of referred candidates got an interview
  against about 3% of cold applicants (partly because employees refer people who already fit).
  But referral policies are for people the employee knows, and on two hiring systems whose help
  pages say so, a referral that arrives *after* you applied stays filed as "applied". So: someone
  who knows you, before applying (needs your OK); everyone else, "could you flag my application?"
  after they reply.
- **Check where the job really is before writing to anyone.** 3 of the 20 jobs tested were not
  where the job board said: "Remote" was "Remote, US", "EMEA" was Ukraine, "London" was Madrid.
  You had already applied to all three.

## The 5-minute routine for one job

### Step 0. Read the posting (about 1 minute, no searches)

You already have it open from applying. Copy out:

- a **named contact**, if any (common in Dutch postings; rare in German posting texts, though
  German JOIN pages have a separate contact field);
- **"reports to ..."** or "you'll work closely with our CTO";
- the **team name** ("Funnel Platform", "Reference Data team");
- the **interview steps**, which tell you who screens first ("call with one of our Talent
  Partners", "Recruiter Screening", "founder interview");
- the **company size** (LinkedIn's company page header, or the posting: "Coworkers 140+");
- the **location line** on the company's own page. Example: a job listed as "Remote" on an
  aggregator said "Remote, US" on the company's page and "does not provide sponsorship for work
  authorizations in the United States".

### Step 1. Free hints on LinkedIn (about 1 minute, no people searches)

- Find the job on LinkedIn Jobs (company + title). If it's there, look at **"Meet the hiring
  team"**: an in-house recruiter or the hiring manager listed there is the best single lead.
- The company page header shows whether you have **1st-degree connections** there.

### Step 2. Pick one person (1-3 minutes, 1-2 people searches)

Today's order (the skill's ladder) stays, but each step gets quicker with the words from step 0:

1. **A** fellow AUB grad, then **B** an engineer with an Arab or Muslim name (your call,
   2026-11-04). One search each.
2. **C**, made faster:
   - a named contact on the posting, or the job poster;
   - 50 people or fewer: the founder or CTO, especially one the posting names;
   - over 50: the hiring manager, found with **one** keyword search using the posting's own
     words ("Cluster to Cluster", "Software Development Manager");
   - the in-house recruiter or talent partner for engineering in that region;
   - a team lead.

What the evidence says about this order (all small or indirect, so treat it as a hint):

- **Recruiters answer candidates for a living.** In a 2025 dataset of 11.5M sales connection
  requests, HR and talent people replied most (10.9%), ahead of C-level (7.0%) and founders
  (6.4%) (grade B, and it is sellers writing to buyers, not job seekers). Your week one points
  the same way: in-house recruiters accepted 4 of 12, hiring managers 3 of 24.
- **The A and B steps are not proven either way.** Notes opening "fellow AUB alum" were
  accepted 1 of 8 and engineers 0 of 4, and since Nov 8 about 4 in 10 notes go to these two
  groups. Eight and four people are far too few to judge a rule you set on purpose.
- **A shared link helps break ties.** In LinkedIn's own experiment on 20 million members
  (Science, 2022), "moderately weak" ties, people you share some connections with but don't know
  well, led to the most job moves, especially in software. So within a step, the person with a few
  mutual connections is a fair pick (the skill already says this). A study of Facebook users found
  one strong tie worth more than one weak tie (Journal of Labor Economics, 2017), which is the case
  for Step R below.

So: keep the order, but **write the contact's type on every card** (`type:recruiter`,
`type:manager`, `type:founder`, `type:engineer`, `type:aub`) so the review in a few weeks can
answer this with your own numbers. If you want to try the recruiter-first order sooner, it is
test T3 in [outreach-experiments.md](outreach-experiments.md) (needs your OK).

### Step 3. Nobody found (about 1 minute, no LinkedIn searches)

- In a normal browser, Google: `site:linkedin.com/in "<Company>" "<title from the posting>"`.
  This is a Google search, so it probably does not use LinkedIn's monthly limit (not verified).
- For companies of 100-1,000 people, theorg.com may name the Head or VP of Engineering. It can be
  out of date.
- Still nobody after 5 minutes: write "no LinkedIn contact" and what you searched in the card's
  Tracking notes (today's rule), and move on.

### Search budget

| step | minutes | LinkedIn people searches |
|---|---|---|
| 0. Posting | ~1 | 0 |
| 1. Job poster, company header | ~1 | 0 (a job search, not a people search) |
| 2. Ladder | 1-3 | 1-2 typical, 3-4 worst case |
| 3. Google fallback | ~1 | 0 |
| **Total** | **3-6** | **1-4** |

At 25-30 jobs a week that is 25-120 people searches a week. The skill's current cap (6 searches
and 5 profiles per company) allows up to about 180. Ways to stay under LinkedIn's limit: read the
B names off a results page you already loaded for A or C, run B as a Google search, or lower the
cap to 3 (each needs your OK, since the cap is in the skill).

## Referrers

**What a referral is worth.** Ashby, a hiring system used mostly by tech companies, published its
own data (grade A):

- referrals were about 1% of applications, and 40% of referred candidates reached an interview,
  against about 3% of people who applied cold (referrals report, May 2025: 38M applications,
  2021-2024);
- referrals were about 18% of hires (inbound report, Oct 2025: about 250,000 hires, 2021 to mid
  2025).

Careful: employees refer people they already think will fit, so part of that gap is who gets
referred, not the referral itself.

**The timing problem.** You apply first, then reach out. What the hiring systems' own help pages
say about a referral that arrives after you applied:

| system | what happens | source |
|---|---|---|
| Ashby | The source stays "Applied". If the company allows it, the referrer's name is added to the application ("Credited To"). The company can also block it. | docs.ashbyhq.com, referrals and referral links (2025-07-10) |
| Workable | Not counted as the referrer's ("you are not the original source"), but their note is shared with the hiring team. | help.workable.com, article 360011239814 |
| Workday | The company can turn on a rule that refuses referrals for someone who already applied. | doc.workday.com, referrals and endorsements |
| Greenhouse, Lever, SmartRecruiters | Not found. | |

Company referral-bonus rules often exclude anyone who applied in the last 6-12 months (policy
templates; one public handbook calls a post-application referral an "endorsement"). So a
stranger you contact after applying can still put in a good word, but usually not a formal
referral.

**Who can actually refer you: someone who knows you.** GitLab's public referral policy defines a
referral as "someone you have worked with in the past, or have developed a close personal
relationship with", and tells staff to turn down strangers who ask on LinkedIn. An AUB alum you
have never met is still a stranger to a policy like that: a good person to write to, but at most
an "endorsement" (a note to the recruiter, no bonus). So a possible referrer is:

- a 1st-degree connection you have actually talked to (the company page header shows them);
- a former colleague, or a classmate you know, who moved there.

For everyone else, the useful ask comes after they reply: "could you flag my application to the
recruiter?" ([message-templates.md](message-templates.md), section 4).

**Step R, only with your OK** (it changes two rules: "apply first" and "never ask for a
referral"):

- Only for jobs graded 4-5 where step 1 shows someone who knows you.
- Message that person **before** applying, with one short, closed question.
- Apply within 48 hours whatever happens. If they offer to refer you, apply through their
  referral link or form.
- That person is the job's one person. No second contact.
- Not for strangers, alumni you don't know included: they mostly can't refer you, and asking
  uses up the one contact.

| Qatar (on-site) | Large groups with HR departments. | **Work week Sunday-Thursday.** Thursday is their Friday. Send follow-ups Monday-Wednesday mornings, their time (needs your OK: it changes the follow-up timing rule). |

| UK (visa list) | Scale-ups: a "Talent Partner" screens first, then the hiring manager. Startups: founder or CTO. | Pick the talent partner when the posting names one as the first step. Agencies can't sponsor a worker they place at a client (UK sponsor guidance, via legal commentary), so only use one whose ad says the client sponsors. |
| Netherlands (visa list) | Vacancies often name a contact person ("Questions? Call or message ..."). | That person comes first, and the vacancy invites questions, so ask one. A vacancy saying "Acquisitie ... wordt niet op prijs gesteld" means "no agencies". |
| Ireland (visa list) | Mid-size: the posting names the manager's title. Big tech: a regional recruiter plus the team's manager. | Use the manager title or team name as the one keyword. At big tech, pick the recruiter covering Dublin or EMEA engineering. |
| Fully remote | 50 or fewer: founder or CTO. Bigger: a recruiter screens first, then an engineering manager. | Check the location line first. Staffing firms posting contract roles: their recruiter is the only door. |

## Agency recruiters

- Week one: 0 of 5 accepted (tiny sample).
- UK: an agency can't sponsor someone it places at a client, so an agency paid on placement has
  little reason to put forward someone who needs a visa. Only worth it when its ad says the client
  sponsors.
- UAE and Qatar: agencies are a big channel for experienced hires. Worth one message when the
  agency posted the job (today's rule). An agency that asks you for money is a red flag in the UAE.
- Staffing firms with contract roles: their recruiter is the one person.

## LinkedIn limits that change the plan

All from LinkedIn help pages as quoted by other sites, so check them in your own account:

| limit | free account | Premium Career |
|---|---|---|
| notes on invitations | 3 a month, 200 characters each (an older page says 5) | unlimited; 300 characters (only an older page says so) |
| people searches | a monthly cap, number not published | reported to have the same cap (only Premium Business, Sales Navigator and Recruiter remove it) |
| invitations | about 100 a week (not published) | same |
| InMail (message to a non-connection) | none | 5 a month; reportedly refunded if they answer within 90 days |
| withdraw an invite | can't re-invite that person for up to 3 weeks | same |

You send 300-character notes, so you are almost certainly on Premium. If you ever drop to a free
account: send most invites without a note and let the follow-up message do the work.

With Premium Career, the 5 InMails a month could go to grade 4-5 jobs whose recruiter is outside
your network or ignored the invite. The credit comes back if they answer, so the cost is small
(an idea to test, not proven).

**The free way to be found instead of finding: "Open to work", visible to recruiters only (your
call).** LinkedIn shows it only to people using LinkedIn Recruiter, and says it takes steps to hide
it from recruiters at your current company but can't guarantee complete privacy (LinkedIn Help,
answer 67405, read through a search snippet, not opened). Set the job titles and places you want (for example Berlin, Munich, Amsterdam, Dubai,
Doha, remote). Count the recruiter messages it brings each week; that's the same count suggested
for LinkedIn posts in [outreach-experiments.md](outreach-experiments.md).

## The test on 20 real openings (2026-11-14)

| what the posting or company site gave | postings |
|---|---|
| a named contact person | 1 of 20 (Netherlands) |
| a role in the hiring chain ("reports to our Lead Backend Engineer", "founder interview", "Talent Partner" screen) | 7 of 20 |
| the team's name | 7 of 20 |
| company size, without LinkedIn | 9 of 20 |
| the person holding that role, still there, and how you're connected | 0 of 20: only LinkedIn shows this |

Other sources were tried and are not worth the time for one opening: engineering blogs (written by
marketing or product people in 3 of 3), GitHub org pages (a 400-person company listed 6
usernames), press releases (fine for founders, which LinkedIn's company page also shows).

**Side finding: check the location line.** Three of the 20 jobs, all already applied to, were
not where the source said:

| source said | the company's own page said |
|---|---|
| Remote | Remote, US (and no US work-visa sponsorship) |
| EMEA | Ukraine |
| London | Madrid (hybrid) |

Two of them are jobs you can't take; the Madrid one could work only with a Spanish sponsor, which
the London listing's evidence (the UK register) says nothing about. Each was an application and a
LinkedIn note spent on the wrong place. The cheapest guard is step 0 above. A code fix (reading
the company's own page before shortlisting) would be a separate change.
