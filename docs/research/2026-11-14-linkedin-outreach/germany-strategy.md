# Germany strategy (2026-11-14)

How to get hired in Germany from Lebanon by contacting the right people, and what to tell them.
Every legal claim was checked against the law text, the Federal Employment Agency's own guidance
or the German embassy in Beirut. Personal details that decide your exact case (degree, dates)
are in a private note in the git-ignored `reports/` folder, not here.

Evidence grades: **A** = law text or an official government page, **B** = a reputable firm,
chamber or large dataset, **C** = blog or anecdote.

## The short version

- **Germany is the best legal fit of the four visa-list countries compared** (Germany, the
  Netherlands, the UK and Ireland; Finland, Spain and Italy weren't checked). There is no sponsor
  licence: the employer signs one Federal Employment Agency form and pays at least about €46k a
  year. The IT route doesn't check degrees at all. The UK needs a licensed sponsor paying about
  £55k for software developers; Ireland needs about €69k without a relevant degree.
- **Your route is the EU Blue Card for IT specialists** (§18g(2) of the Residence Act): 3 years
  of IT experience in the last 7, no degree check. You meet the 3 years on the calendar; the
  Employment Agency may not count a first, junior year (details in the private note). If it
  doesn't, a second permit for experienced IT workers needs only 2 years. **The employer's side
  is the same on both**, so that's what your messages should promise.
- **The problem isn't the law, it's the postings.** Over the last month the tool kept 23 German
  target jobs, dropped about 180 that ask for German, and dropped about 180 more that say nothing
  about visas, which in Germany often just means nobody wrote it down. Only about 20 refused.
- **So the plan:** apply to the German jobs that offer a visa or relocation; ask first at
  English-speaking tech companies whose postings say nothing (needs your OK); write to the founder
  or CTO at small companies, and the recruiter at bigger ones.
- **Get ready without tipping anyone off:** draft the text of a reference letter now, and ask your
  current employer for it only when a German employer moves toward an offer (section 5).

## 1. Why Germany is different

- **No sponsor list.** The UK, the Netherlands and Ireland publish which companies sponsor work
  permits, and the tool uses those lists. Germany has nothing to publish: there is no licence.
  The Employment Agency approves each job, one at a time (§§18-18g, 39, 81a of the Residence Act;
  §§6 and 36 of the Employment Regulation; A).
- **So silence means less in Germany.** Over the last month (Oct 14 - Nov 14), German
  target-role postings in the tool ended up as:

| outcome | postings |
|---|---|
| kept: offers a visa or help moving | 23 |
| dropped: says nothing about visas | about 180 |
| dropped: asks for German, or is written in German | about 180 |
| dropped: refuses sponsorship or wants a right to work | about 20 |

- **German is the bigger wall.** 47% of companies with IT openings say they fail to fill them
  because candidates lack German (Bitkom 2026 report, p. 10, 319 companies with IT openings, B).
  Only 6% of companies recruit more from abroad to fill IT gaps (Bitkom press release, Sep 2026).
- **Moving is a plus, but not the main worry.** 42% of those companies struggle with candidates
  who won't move (same report, p. 10), and you would. For a hire from outside the EU, though, the
  bigger worry is the visa, which section 8 answers.

A hand-read of 18 of the silent postings: about half are real options for you (English team,
your kind of stack, no security clearance). The rest are defence work needing clearance, C++ or
ML-research roles, or have no text at all.

## 2. Your permit route

### The IT Blue Card (§18g(2) of the Residence Act)

What the law asks (A, gesetze-im-internet.de, read 2026-11-14):

- a job in IT: ISCO-08 group 25 (ICT professionals, which includes software developers) or 133
  (ICT service managers). Backend, full-stack and team-lead developer jobs are group 25;
- **at least 3 years of experience in such a job, gained in the last 7 years**, at a level
  "comparable to a university degree";
- a salary of at least 45.3% of the pension ceiling: **€45,934.20 a year in 2026**;
- the Employment Agency's approval (always needed on this route);
- a contract of at least 6 months;
- **no degree check, no recognition paperwork, no German.**

**Your case:** on the calendar you meet the 3 years. The risk is in the words "comparable to a
university degree". The Employment Agency's guidance gives
an example where only the later, more senior years counted and the early ones didn't (Fachliche
Weisungen, Dec 2024, FW 18g.0.10, A). If it decides your first, junior year doesn't count, you
fall short of 3 years. Every month you work makes the case stronger.

**Remote work counts.** The law says nothing about where the experience was gained, and for the
fallback route the guidance says outright that experience abroad counts (FW 19c.6.3, A). Whether
contractor work counts the same as employment: not verified.

### The fallback: the experienced-professional permit for IT (§19c(2) with §6 Employment Regulation)

- 2 years of experience in the last 5 that prepares you for the job;
- a salary of at least 45% of the pension ceiling: **€45,630 in 2026**;
- for IT jobs, **no qualification at all is needed** (§6(1) sentence 3);
- Employment Agency approval; language is not checked; no test of whether a local could do the
  job;
- the fast track (below) works for it too.

The cost of landing here instead: it's an ordinary residence permit, not a Blue Card, so the
faster path to permanent residence (27 months with basic German, or 21 with B1, §18c(2)) is for
Blue Card holders only; the fallback follows the general rules.

**Old pages are wrong about this route.** Some still describe the 2020-2023 version, which needed
B1 German and 3 years. The current text has neither.

### What doesn't fit

- **The standard Blue Card** (€50,700 in 2026) needs a degree that fits the job. The IT route
  doesn't look at degrees at all, so it's the one to plan on.
- **The Opportunity Card** (Chancenkarte: a year in Germany to look for work) needs points you
  don't have without German, or a degree rated equal to a German one first. It also needs €1,091
  a month in a blocked bank account (about €13,100 for a year) and doesn't fit "remote interviews
  only". A last resort, not a plan.
- Details for your exact case, including an optional degree check that would open two more
  routes, are in the private note.

### The pay floors

| route | 2026 (official) | 2027 (expected, not official) |
|---|---|---|
| standard Blue Card | €50,700 | about €53,100 |
| IT Blue Card (§18g(2)) | **€45,934.20** | about €48,100 |
| experienced IT professional (§19c(2)) | **€45,630** | about €47,800 |

- 2026: the Interior Ministry's notice in the Federal Gazette (BAnz AT 18.12.2025 B3, A) and the
  Hamburg Welcome Center's table (July 2026, A).
- 2027: the government announced next year's pension ceiling of €106,200 on 12 Oct 2026 (the
  upper house still has to approve). The 2027 column is that times the percentages; the official figures come
  by 31 Dec 2026. A job agreed now will probably get its visa decision in 2027.
- **In messages, never quote a euro figure.** Say "a salary above the IT Blue Card floor" (the plain "Blue Card floor" is the higher, standard one).
- **Pay is almost never the problem.** The Employment Agency's own pay data (Entgeltatlas, 2025,
  A) puts the median for expert-level software development at €6,301 a month in Germany, €6,913
  in Berlin and €7,284 in Munich. Even the bottom quarter is far above the floor. Watch only for a
  small startup offering a low salary plus equity.

## 3. What the employer has to do, cost and time

- **No sponsor licence** (A: none exists in the Residence Act or the Employment Regulation).
- **One form:** the "Erklärung zum Beschäftigungsverhältnis" (declaration on the job: duties, pay,
  hours, workplace), downloadable from the Employment Agency. Both sides also sign a short
  statement that the job is real. That's what goes to the Employment Agency.
- **Duties after hiring:** check the permit before day one, keep a copy, tell the foreigners'
  office within 4 weeks if the job ends early (§4a(5) of the Residence Act, A).
- **No levy.** Unlike the UK, there is no skills charge.
- **The optional fast track** ("beschleunigtes Fachkräfteverfahren", §81a of the Residence Act,
  A): the employer files with its local foreigners' office, with your power of attorney, and pays
  **€411**. Then:
  - the Employment Agency's approval counts as given if it doesn't answer within **1 week**
    (2 weeks without the fast track), unless it asks for more information (§36(2) Employment
    Regulation, A);
  - the embassy offers an appointment within **3 weeks** of the pre-approval and usually decides
    within **3 weeks** of a complete application (§31a of the Residence Regulation, A);
  - the foreigners' office's own step has **no deadline**.
- **Total time from signed offer to first day: about 2-4 months, an estimate.** A chamber of
  commerce says about 4 months including degree recognition (IHK Ostthüringen, May 2026, B), and
  your route needs no recognition. No official statistic exists. Don't promise a date.

## 4. The embassy in Beirut

- **Work visas, Blue Card included, are applied for online** through the Foreign Office's portal
  (Auslandsportal): upload, wait for the completeness check, then book your own appointment and
  bring the originals (embassy page, 15 Jan 2026, A). VFS handles Schengen and student visas,
  not work visas.
- **Which portal category to pick** for a no-degree IT Blue Card isn't stated anywhere public.
  A wrong category gets the upload deleted. **Ask the embassy through its contact form first.**
- **Waiting times** show only inside a logged-in portal account. Not verified.
- **No informal appeal any more:** since 1 July 2025, a refusal can only be challenged in court
  in Berlin, in German (embassy page, 25 Jun 2025, A). So the first application must be complete.
- **Paperwork:** Arabic documents need a sworn German translation; certificates generally have to
  be legalised; the fee is €75, paid in US dollars (embassy leaflet, Jan 2024, A).
- **Not verified:** whether Lebanese applications go through an extra security check (§73 of the
  Residence Act) that adds time. The list of countries isn't public.

## 5. What to prepare now

1. **The text of a reference letter, drafted but not requested yet:** each job title with start
   and end dates, full-time hours, the main duties (backend, AWS, leading 3 engineers), and that
   the work was done remotely from Beirut, to be translated into German by a sworn translator.
   **Ask your current employer for it only when a German employer moves toward an offer:** asking
   now tells them you're leaving. For the IT Blue Card, the Employment Agency's guidance asks for
   past employers' references only "if needed" (FW 18g.0.12, A); for the fallback permit it
   expects confirmation of the experience (BA guidance, A), and Lower Saxony's fast-track
   checklist for IT specialists lists references (A). Either way, it's needed only after an offer.
2. Contracts or pay records, if you have them, and any training certificates.
3. A one-paragraph visa note for recruiters, built from the lines in section 8.

## 6. The market for an English speaker

- **A slower market with a real gap.** Software job ads on Indeed Germany fell 18.8% in 2025
  (Indeed Hiring Lab, Dec 2025, B). Unfilled IT jobs: 79,000 in 2026, down from 109,000 in 2025
  and 149,000 in 2023 (Bitkom, Sep 2026, B). It took companies 7.7 months on average to fill an IT
  job (Bitkom, Jan 2026, B), so a few days spent asking before applying costs little.
- **AI is where the growth is:** 20.8% of software ads mentioned AI by Nov 2025 (Indeed).
- **Where:** of the silent English postings in the tool, 35% were in Berlin and 24% in Munich;
  Hamburg was small.
- **"Remote" in a German posting usually means remote inside Germany** ("You need to live in
  Germany for this role"). Treat these as move-to-Germany jobs: the Blue Card works for them.
  Working for a German company while living in Lebanon would need a foreign payroll; no source
  says that's common.
- **Apply as mid-level or team lead, not junior:** 55% of companies expect AI to remove classic
  entry-level tasks (Bitkom 2026).

## 7. Which openings, and who to contact

### In this order

1. **German postings that offer a visa or relocation** (the visa list), minus the two problems in
   section 11. Apply, then the invite note. The German line goes only in B notes while test T1
   runs, or in every German note if you OK it (README, decisions).
2. **Small AI and software startups (10-60 people) in Berlin and Munich whose postings are in
   English and say nothing about visas.** Write to the CTO or a founder first (section 9).
3. **Silent postings at companies whose text signals international hiring** ("60+
   nationalities", "international team", "English is our working language"). The recruiter first.
4. **Skip:** postings that ask for German (read the whole posting: one misspelled "required"
   slipped past the tool), postings with a degree requirement you don't meet, defence or export-controlled
   work (security clearance), and postings that have closed.

### Who to contact, by size

| company | first | second |
|---|---|---|
| under about 50 people | the CTO or a founder. A JOIN job page may name a contact person in its own field; the Impressum names the managing directors | an engineer on the team |
| scale-up | the recruiter for that role | the engineering manager of the team the posting names |
| large | the recruiter, after applying | someone who knows you there, for a referral |

**German sources that point at the decision-maker:**

- **The Impressum.** German law requires every business website to name the people who represent
  the company; for a GmbH, the managing directors (§5 of the Digital Services Act, DDG, which
  replaced §5 TMG on 14 May 2024; A). At a 10-40 person startup, those 1-3 people are the founders,
  and one of them decides on hiring. Every German Impressum that loaded in the test named them;
  companies run from a foreign parent (a US Inc., a UK Ltd) had none.
- **The commercial register** (handelsregister.de) is free since 1 Aug 2022 and shows current
  managing directors.
- **JOIN job pages** (join.com) have a structured "contact person" field; one test posting named
  its Co-Founder and CTO there. Personio, Greenhouse and Ashby postings in the test named nobody.
- **LinkedIn over XING:** 15% of German adults use LinkedIn against 8% for XING (YouGov, Feb 2026,
  B); XING had more users until the end of 2021. Use XING only for older managers at traditional
  German firms.

**In the test of 15 German postings:** none named a contact in the posting text; one named the
CTO in JOIN's contact field; the Impressum gave the directors for every German company whose page
loaded.

## 8. What the first message must answer

The worry behind silence from a German company: "a visa from outside the EU is cost, paperwork
and months". Your answers, all true on both permit routes:

| they think | the fact | your line |
|---|---|---|
| "We don't sponsor visas." | Germany has no sponsor licence; the employer signs one form | "Germany has no sponsor licence. You'd sign one Federal Employment Agency form, and I'd apply for the permit myself." |
| "What about your degree?" | the IT route checks experience, not degrees | "There's an IT route that looks at experience instead of a degree, and a fallback for IT jobs that needs no qualification at all. Either way it's the same form for you." |
| "It's expensive." | no levy; the €411 fast track is optional | "There's no levy. The only government fee on your side is €411, and only if you choose the fast track." |
| "It takes too long." | fast track: 1 week for the Employment Agency, 3 weeks for an embassy appointment | "The fast track gives the Employment Agency one week and the embassy three weeks for an appointment." |
| "We need German." | neither permit requires German | "Neither permit needs German." |
| "Will they really move?" | you would | "I'd move for this role." |
| "Can we interview remotely?" | the company decides; some want an on-site round | answer from your profile (see below); add "Beirut is one hour ahead of Berlin." |
| "Is there a salary threshold?" | about €46k in 2026, updated each January | "The floor is well below normal developer pay; it's updated every January." |
| "Do we need to check your degree?" | not on the IT route | "Not on the IT route: it looks at experience, shown through employer references." |

- These lines use only what the employer must do and where you would move. If they ask about
  your working language, video interviews or notice period, answer from your profile: that's
  answering a question, not pitching.
- **Don't** quote €45,934.20, promise a start date, or claim a number of years "at degree level":
  the Employment Agency decides that.
- **Order in a first message:** your fit in one line (the two achievements), then the visa line,
  then one question. The ready-made notes are in [message-templates.md](message-templates.md).
- **Tone:** plain facts and a direct question read as normal in Germany, not rude (Erin Meyer's
  culture framework, C). No flattery. Startups use first names.
- **Expect the salary question.** German forms often require a number (one test posting's form had
  a required "What is your desired salary?"). Germany missed the 7 June 2026 deadline for the EU
  pay-transparency law (GvW law firm, Aug 2026, B), so employers may still ask. Have a range ready
  that starts well above the floor.
- **No photo, no date of birth** on the CV: German anti-discrimination law (AGG, A) covers age and
  origin, and some postings ask you to leave them out. Cover letters are fading in tech postings
  ("You don't need a cover letter").

## 9. Ask before applying (needs your OK)

For the silent postings in groups 2 and 3 of section 7. Your rule today: a German job counts only
with clear sponsorship. Asking first keeps that rule: you apply only after a yes.

- **Which:** German location, rejected as "no visa evidence", relevance "match", posted in the last
  7 days, not at a company that refused a visa elsewhere, not asking for German or a clearance.
  Each Monday, the 10 newest, one per company (query Q6 in
  [outreach-experiments.md](outreach-experiments.md)). The tool finds 13-27 such postings a week.
- **Who:** the CTO or a founder at small companies, the recruiter at bigger ones (section 7).
- **What:** the "before I apply" note in [message-templates.md](message-templates.md): one
  question (do you hire from outside the EU for it?) and the employer facts.
- **A variant, also your call:** where applying is one click, apply the same day and send the
  normal note with the German line, instead of waiting for the answer. Where it takes a long form,
  a take-home task or an on-site round, ask first.
- **After a yes:** apply, then the short reply in
  [message-templates.md](message-templates.md#6-short-answers-to-the-usual-replies). The ask was
  your first message, so that reply is the second; at most one more.
- **Count it as its own funnel**, decided on yes-counts per invite after 30 invites (details in
  [outreach-experiments.md](outreach-experiments.md)): 3 or more yes → bring you a proposal to
  change the German rule; 0 yes → stop; 1-2 → 30 more.
- **If LinkedIn is too slow** (few accepts), write to the managing director named in the
  Impressum (small companies) or the JOIN contact person instead. German posting texts rarely name
  anyone.

## 10. Germany against three of your other visa-list countries

Only what changes the decision. Finland, Spain and Italy weren't checked; the EU Blue Card rules
behind Germany's IT route may give them something similar.

| country, route | degree needed? | pay floor (2026) | employer's burden | verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Germany**, IT Blue Card or the fallback | no | about €46k a year | no licence; one form; optional €411 fast track | **best legal fit**; the catch is few postings say so, and many want German |
| **Netherlands**, highly skilled migrant | no (a degree only lowers the floor for recent graduates) | €4,357 a month under 30, €5,942 at 30 or over, holiday pay not counted | must be an IND recognised sponsor (public list) | **strong**, if the salary band fits; the tool already checks the list |
| **UK**, Skilled Worker | no; the *job* must be degree level (changed 22 July 2025) | the higher of £41,700 or the going rate, **£54,700 for software developers** (less if under 26) | sponsor licence + Immigration Skills Charge (£480 or £1,320 a year); English B2 since 8 Jan 2026 | possible, but the most expensive for the employer |
| **Ireland**, Critical Skills permit | yes, for the lower floor | €40,904 with a relevant degree; **€68,911 without** | 2-year offer; at least half the company's staff from the EEA; €1,000 fee | hard |

Sources: IND income requirements and business.gov.nl (A); gov.uk Skilled Worker pages and the
skills charge (A); enterprise.gov.ie, Critical Skills Employment Permit (A); all read 2026-11-14.

**What this means for the visa list:** 345 of its jobs are in the UK, 108 in the Netherlands and
25 in Germany. The UK is where most of your visa-list effort goes, and it's the hardest route for
an employer. More effort on Germany and the Netherlands matches where the law is easiest.

## 11. Problems in the tool's German jobs (for your call; nothing changed)

- **Clera is a recruiting intermediary, not an employer.** It accounts for 5 of the 23 kept German
  jobs (296 Clera rows in the database: 290 rejected, 5 on the visa list, 1 shortlisted). Its
  postings describe an unnamed client ("an early-stage logistics technology team"), and its own
  pages call it "The intro company" and offer "$5,000 per hire". The agency rules don't catch it.
  Adding it to the agency or blocked list would be your call.
- **"Relocation within the EU" was read as relocation help.** prefix.dev is on the visa list for
  "We help with relocation within the EU", which does nothing for someone moving from Lebanon.
- **A typo let German requirements through the language check.** Two Tulip postings say "Fluency
  in English & German is reuired". They were dropped anyway (no visa evidence), but they would
  show up in the "ask before applying" list. Read before asking.
- **Closed postings stay listed.** Two kept German jobs had closed (their ids are gone from the
  company boards) by Nov 14.
- **LinkedIn isn't searched for Germany.** The LinkedIn source covers the UAE, Qatar, Lebanon and
  the UK and the Netherlands with "visa sponsorship"; Germany could be added the same way
  (decision 17 keeps that source small, so it's your call).

## 12. People who might help

- **AUB's alumni association had a Germany chapter** (founded around 2012, per AUB's MainGate
  magazine, A). Whether it's active now is behind the alumni login: check it there, or ask AUB
  alumni relations.
- **About 155,000 people of Lebanese descent live in Germany** (2021 figures; Arab Reform
  Initiative, Aug 2023, B), Berlin above all. The groups named are religious and cultural, not
  professional.
- **No Lebanese or Arab tech-professional network in Germany could be verified.** The practical
  route is LinkedIn's alumni search: AUB, filtered to people living in Germany.
- **Referral programmes are common:** in a 2023 survey of 335 companies worldwide, 74% had one
  and 95% of those paid a cash bonus, mostly €501-1,000 (Radancy, reported by Personalwirtschaft,
  B). A referral is only possible from someone who knows you (see
  [contact-discovery.md](contact-discovery.md#referrers)).
